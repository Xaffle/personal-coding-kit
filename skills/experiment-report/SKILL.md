# Experiment report

Help the user plan an experiment or produce and review a report suited to its discipline, stage, audience, and available evidence. Follow the user's requested scope and outline. Do not import project-specific assumptions or require every experiment to use a control group, a statistical test, software, or a fixed report structure.

## Workflow

1. **Determine the task.** Identify whether the user needs a plan, a report of completed or ongoing work, an audit, or a revision. Establish the research question, intended reader, deliverable, applicable local instructions, and whether an existing report must be preserved.
2. **Inspect available material.** Depending on the study, examine protocols, raw observations, instruments and calibration records, datasets, implementation, configurations, analysis scripts, logs, or version history. Treat earlier summaries and screenshots as claims to check against primary records when available. For external research claims, verify reliable primary sources and cite them near the claims they support.
3. **Describe the design and method.** Explain the question, hypotheses where applicable, experimental units, selection, variables, conditions or comparisons, procedure, and analysis. State design choices and constraints that affect interpretation; identify omissions and reasons rather than inventing them. For plans, distinguish proposed steps from decisions already made.
4. **Define measures and analysis.** As applicable, give each measure's meaning, unit, reference, calculation, numerator and denominator, aggregation, and analysis population. Describe repetitions, uncertainty, and statistical methods when relevant. Keep measures with different populations, purposes, or versions distinct; do not present a proxy as an effect it does not measure.
5. **Report findings at evidence strength.** Relate material claims to identifiable evidence or explicitly state their evidence status. Distinguish observations, analysis, and interpretation; disclose relevant sample counts, exclusions, failures, missing results, versions, and conditions. Distinguish completed runs, reanalysis, reused or historical output, proposed work, and unknowns when relevant. Do not invent results, infer causation without an appropriate basis, or claim broader validity than the design supports.
6. **Handle review and revision.** Identify the concrete concern, recheck its evidence, and change the requested scope. Recheck affected figures, tables, summaries, conclusions, and citations. Preserve the original when requested. Mark unavailable or inapplicable information with a reason when needed; omit irrelevant sections instead of filling a template.
7. **Review the deliverable.** Check the consistency of design, versions, units, formulas, calculations, figures, citations, and conclusions. Follow local writing and format requirements. Explain methods and limitations in terms the intended reader can follow. Report material evidence gaps and checks performed in the handoff.

For a prospective experiment, describe the intended procedure and analysis as plans, label unresolved choices, and do not present expected observations as results. For an ongoing study, separate completed observations from future work.

## Completion criteria

The deliverable answers the requested question at the current study stage. Material claims have traceable support or an explicit evidence status; measures and relevant conditions are defined; and limitations and outstanding unknowns are visible. Conclusions do not exceed the evidence or study design.
